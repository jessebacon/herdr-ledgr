# Park the question, protect the trunk

The best-supported non-linear alternative to linear AI chat is not a canvas. It is a linear trunk with four cheap side moves around it: a side question that never enters history, a question you park while the model is still talking, a branch that opens on demand from an exact copy of the trunk, and one short, human-approved "confluence" message that carries the branch's answer back to the end of the trunk. Discourse theory strongly supports the premise: conversation behaves like a stack of open questions inside a mostly tree-shaped record. Memory research shows that deferred intentions fade within seconds, so a reader working through a 600-word AI answer will lose follow-up questions. The interface evidence is weaker than the premise. Branching and canvas tools reliably widen exploration, but the studies are small (6 to 24 participants), several find no cognitive-load benefit, and users keep chat as their main surface. The constructal, river-basin framing has no published application to discourse. Treat it as a design metaphor. Its one durable lesson is that a tributary delivers its water, not its whole watershed: the trunk should receive a branch's result, not its transcript. Claude Code already ships four branching commands with distinct meanings (`/btw`, `/branch`, `/fork` and `/subtask`, plus the Agent tool's fork mode), which puts it ahead of every chat app. Even so, neither it nor any competitor lets you pin a question to a passage mid-answer, records branch lineage in a documented field, shows what a branch knows, or merges a `/branch` back. Herdr can host that missing layer. It already draws a parent-child agent tree, launches Claude with arbitrary `argv`, and has a free slot in the right-hand panel. Its one weak link is that it has no notion of a Claude session id. Build order: a session resolver and branch launcher first, a question inbox that opens at the end of each turn second, confluence third, and the graph view last.

## Theory treats conversation as a stack inside a mostly-tree

Nearly every formal model of discourse is hierarchical, so the premise that conversation is non-linear is mainstream, not fringe. Grosz and Sidner model attention as a **focus stack**: opening a discourse segment pushes a focus space, satisfying its purpose pops it, a digression is a push, and cue phrases such as "but anyway" signal the pop back ([Grosz & Sidner 1986](https://aclanthology.org/J86-3001/)). Roberts puts a **stack of questions under discussion (QUD)** at the center. Once participants accept a question, they are committed to answering it until it is answered or judged unanswerable, and moves that ignore the top question are non-sequiturs ([Roberts 2012](https://doi.org/10.3765/sp.5.6)). Ginzburg's KoS loosens the stack into a partially ordered set, so several questions can be open at once ([Ginzburg 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199697922.001.0001)). Conversation analysis finds the same nesting in recorded talk: insertion sequences open inside an unfinished exchange and can contain further insertions before the talk returns ([Schegloff 2007](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511791208)). Levinson goes further. He argues that dialogue routinely nests deeper than syntax ever does, and that the action-planning system's **stack of subgoals** underlies this ([Levinson 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/recursion-in-pragmatics/89BE56395B7811B32DAD3F830AE9D390)). Legate, Pesetsky and Yang reply that those claims are "at best unwarranted" ([Legate et al. 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/recursive-misrepresentations-a-reply-to-levinson-2013/234B497EB948723F05548AFEC6418678)).

The corpus evidence says "mostly hierarchical, with real exceptions." SDRT's right-frontier constraint is a stack-like rule: new material may attach only to the most recent unit or the units above it. In roughly 100 annotated documents it held **about 95% of the time** ([Afantenos & Asher 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.5880)). Wolf and Gibson, however, found so many crossed dependencies and multi-parent nodes in 135 texts that **about 12.5% of relations** would have to be deleted to force a tree ([Wolf & Gibson 2005](https://doi.org/10.1162/0891201054223977); figures via [Egg & Redeker 2010](https://preview.aclanthology.org/naacl24-info/L10-1547)). Egg and Redeker counter that Wolf and Gibson's annotation protocol invited crossings. Even trained annotators agree only moderately when building QUD trees (**Cohen's κ = 0.52**) ([Fu 2025 survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15573)). Most of this corpus work studies written monologue, not dialogue. It shows that discourse can be described as a tree, not that speakers hold a tree in their heads.

The closest empirical analogue to "cataloging questions while the speaker talks" is TED-Q. Readers moving through TED transcripts two sentences at a time produced **2,412 questions**. The mean rating for whether a question got answered was **2.50 on a 1-5 scale**, and only **367 were fully answered** in the next stretch of text. The authors read this as listeners anticipating the speaker's next moves ([Westera, Mayol & Rohde 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.141/)). Listeners generate questions continuously, and most stay open locally. Text chat makes the same point from the other side: multi-party channels interleave several conversations that must be "disentangled" into reply graphs ([Kummerfeld et al. 2019](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1374/)).

Two consequences shape everything below. First, the stack and the tree are different objects. The stack is attentional: which questions are open right now. The tree, more precisely a mostly-tree graph with cross-links, is the record of everything opened, answered or abandoned. A linear chat transcript flattens that structure into one line and shows neither, so the user carries the stack in their head. Second, text already offers what speech lacks: Clark and Brennan list reviewability and revisability among the properties of written media ([Clark & Brennan 1991](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006)). The case against linear chat is therefore about structure and navigation, meaning finding and returning to open questions, not about raw memory for what was said.

Constructal law fits only as a metaphor. Bejan's principle says that flow systems evolve shapes that make flow easier, which yields river basins and lungs ([Bejan & Lorente 2010](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0302)). Its nearest extension to ideas treats citation curves as an idea spreading through a population ([Bejan & Lorente 2012](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/760133)). That is about diffusion, not discourse structure, and a targeted search found **no application of constructal law to conversation, discourse, hypertext or chat**. Its standing as a law is also contested: hydrology commenters argued that it lacks a stated domain and a rule for applying it ([HESS-D discussion](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/3/S499/2006/hessd-3-S499-2006.pdf)). The metaphor breaks topologically too. Tributaries start outside the main channel and converge on it, while conversational branches split from the trunk and later rejoin it. That pattern matches an **anabranching** river, a channel that splits around an island and rejoins downstream ([Nanson](https://ro.uow.edu.au/smhpapers/1940)). Conversations also cross-reference sideways and reopen settled questions, which water cannot do. If you need a non-metaphorical efficiency argument, the peer-reviewed route is information-theoretic work on how efficiency shapes language ([Gibson et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.02.003)). Matuschak's design argument that associative networks beat strict hierarchies also cuts against any pure-tree picture ([Matuschak](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Prefer_associative_ontologies_to_hierarchical_taxonomies)). One actionable lesson survives all of this: **a confluence delivers the tributary's water, not its watershed**. The trunk should receive a branch's result and never its transcript.

## Deferred questions decay in seconds, and long AI turns multiply them

No experiment directly measures listeners forgetting their own questions while a speaker keeps talking, but adjacent findings converge. The focus of attention holds **about four chunks** ([Cowan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922)). In one set of experiments, people had to carry out an intention after a delay of 5, 15 or 40 seconds. They forgot it quickly when the task was demanding or the delay was interrupted, at rates the authors call significant for real-world settings, and neither rehearsal nor implementation intentions prevented it ([Einstein et al. 2003](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Einstein-JEPA03.pdf)). Suspended goals lose activation unless they are rehearsed or cued ([Altmann & Trafton 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2)). Interruptions averaging **2.8 seconds doubled** errors in keeping one's place in a sequence, and **4.4-second** interruptions tripled them ([Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986)). "Tip of the tongue" is the wrong label for the experience. That phenomenon is a failure to retrieve a known word ([Brown & McNeill 1966](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371%2866%2980040-3)), whereas losing a question is a failure of prospective memory, the memory for things you intend to do.

For speech, the evidence firmly supports the idea that listeners work while the speaker is still talking, though researchers debate the timing. Gaps between turns run **about 200 ms**, while encoding even one word takes **600 ms or more**, so listeners must plan their replies during the incoming turn. Levinson and Torreira call conversation "constant double tasking" ([Levinson & Torreira 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731)). EEG shows planning starting midway through a question ([Bögels, Magyari & Levinson 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep12881)). Dual-task data suggest it starts late ([Sjerps & Meyer 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.10.008)). Early planning measurably costs comprehension of the turn still in progress ([Bögels, Casillas & Levinson 2018](https://chatterlab.uchicago.edu/lab-publications/Bogels_et_al_2018_Planning_versus_comprehension_in_turn_taking_Neuropsychologia.pdf)). No study has tested the more specific claim that listeners "bundle context" around each pending question. The analogy also has an honest limit: text chat is asynchronous. A reader can pause and re-read, so the 200 ms pressure disappears. What carries over is the work of holding and ordering several questions, not the timing stress.

AI turns make that holding problem worse in one specific way. Human turns have a median length of about **1.2 seconds** ([Levinson & Torreira 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731)). Model turns run to hundreds of words, and the user cannot take the floor without stopping generation, so several questions pile up while a linear thread lets only one go next. Users say so. In Graphologue's early interview study, participants found answers long-winded, hit "Stop generating", and wanted to ask "what does this line mean?" about a specific line ([Jiang et al. 2023](https://hci.ucsd.edu/papers/graphologue.pdf)). Seven of eight ExploreLLM participants called ChatGPT's responses verbose or generic and described digging back through a wall of text ([Ma et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00763)). A log study of about 84,000 finance and health conversations found that length interacts with density. In finance, longer answers drew fewer clarification requests when information density was low and more when it was high, crossing over at **roughly 150-200 words**. Heavier markdown predicted more repeated prompting (**odds ratio ≈ 1.1**) ([Wang, Bilal & Zaman 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17301v1)). The same study found that the number of topics in a conversation did **not** predict breakdowns, a mild counterpoint to the idea that multi-topic chat is inherently overloaded. Nobody has measured how many follow-up questions users intend to ask but never do. "Firehose" is practitioners' vocabulary, with no paper behind it.

The strongest argument for branching comes from an asymmetry, not from cognitive load. Between humans, interrupting costs the speaker their place, and deferring a question costs the listener the question. Side sequences and insertion sequences are the culturally evolved compromise: a short, bounded detour with a conventional way back. A model pays no cost for losing its place, so the dilemma becomes one-sided: **a fork is a free interruption**. Two cautions temper this. Capturing a question is itself work: taking notes while listening costs about as much effort as composing text ([Piolat, Olive & Kellogg 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1086)), so capture has to cost one keystroke. And the price of conclusion-heavy output may be disengagement as much as memory. Among 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking ([Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)), and students using LLMs spent less effort but reasoned less deeply than students using web search ([Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/114673/114673.pdf)). Linear accumulation hurts the model as well. Top LLMs scored **39% worse on average** in multi-turn than in single-turn settings, because they lock onto early assumptions and do not recover ([Laban et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)). That is an argument for isolating branches, not just drawing them.

## Branching interfaces widen exploration but have not proven lighter

Independent teams keep finding the same pain in linear chat. In CanvasConvo's early interviews with heavy users, **6 of 8** kept separate documents to preserve outputs and **7 of 8** struggled to explore alternatives without copy-pasting context. One kept 15 to 20 ChatGPT tabs open, and another described their history as "a graveyard of half-finished ideas" ([Amin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15848v2)). Sensecape's authors note that conversation's linear structure limits complex information work ([Suh et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11483)), and OnGoal found that users lose track of their goals as chats grow ([Coscia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21061)). The same qualitative finding from teams in Munich, UCSD, Google and Georgia Tech carries more weight than any single study.

The quantitative results are thinner and split.

| System (status) | Participants and baseline | What improved | What did not |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Sensecape](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11483) (UIST 2023, peer-reviewed) | 12, chat plus a basic concept-map canvas | 68.3 vs 22.8 concepts explored; 12.8 vs 0.7 revisits | Baseline easier to navigate; hierarchy view overwhelmed some users |
| [VisCanvas](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21886) (preprint, 2026) | 20, linear chat on the same backend | Pursuing parallel directions (p = .01) | No workload (NASA-TLX) difference; targeted tasks split 8 vs 10 in chat's favor |
| [Mindalogue](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10570v1) (preprint) | 16, linear chat | Slightly higher task-support ratings | Slower (10.16 vs 7.78 min), more actions, harder to learn (p = .008) |
| [Branchat](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772363.3798792) (CHI 2026 extended abstract) | 12, linear chat | Faster, lower cognitive load, higher trust ("preliminary"; abstract only) | Full results not accessible |
| [CanvasConvo](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15848v2) (preprint, 5-day field study) | 24, no baseline | Usability score (SUS) 78.9; 96% kept an overview | Unsure what a branch knows; chat stayed primary |
| [Contextify](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07121) (preprint) | 6, ChatGPT | SUS 72.08 | No statistics; branching added load; not worth it for short tasks |
| [DirectGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.03691v2) (CHI 2024; direct manipulation, not branching) | 12, ChatGPT replica | About 50% faster; SUS 92 vs 53 | Experienced users only |

The research notes disagree on cognitive load, and that disagreement is itself the finding. Branchat (12 participants, abstract only) and MindTrellis (abstract only) report lower load ([MindTrellis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23129)). The better-controlled comparisons point the other way or nowhere. VisCanvas found no NASA-TLX difference on any dimension. A CHI 2024 sensemaking tool found no load difference against a linear baseline ([Gero et al. 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642139)). Mindalogue's own results contradict its abstract's claim of fewer steps ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10570)). Contextify participants said branching added load, and Spellburst users rated mental demand as their highest workload subscale ([Angert et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.03921)). No study combines an objective load measure, more than 30 participants and a randomized linear control. The defensible claim is that branching addresses a well-documented navigation and organization problem. That it reduces cognitive load is plausible and unproven.

Two design facts survive the noise. First, the benefit depends on the task. Structure pays off for open-ended, divergent work: Sensecape's breadth, VisCanvas's parallel exploration, and a node-tree study that favored structure for brainstorming and chat for step-by-step queries ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06457)). For targeted work, structure costs time. Second, people keep chat. In CanvasConvo's five-day deployment, chat drew **47.2%** of logged events against the canvas's **28.3%**. The 24 participants made **79 branches (3.29 each on average)** and never nested more than **two levels** deep. One participant never adopted the canvas "due to force of habits", and another said, "I didn't know what the branch knows and what it doesn't know" ([Amin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15848v2)). That quote names the new burden branching creates. Non-linear conversation may be natural, but a non-linear interface is not free. Its costs concentrate in two places users notice: what a branch inherits, and how its answer comes back.

## Claude ships four branch verbs; rivals mostly ship copies

### Exact semantics in Claude Code, November 2026

Claude Code splits branching into separate commands that differ on exactly those two axes. The table reflects the documentation as read on 5 November 2026, covering v2.1.311 through v2.1.318.

| Command | What it creates | What it inherits | What comes back | Persistence and caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `/btw <question>`; Desktop side chat (Cmd+;) | An answer in a dismissible overlay | Everything so far except the reply still streaming; no tool access | Nothing; never enters history | Works mid-response; `f` promotes it to a forked subagent (local sessions only); Desktop side chats are not saved to disk |
| `/branch [name]`; `--resume <id> --fork-session` | A new session ID you switch into | Full history to this point; permission grants within the same process; in-flight background agents report into the branch | Nothing; return with `/resume` | No merge; `--fork-session` asks you to re-approve grants, does not restore plan mode, and starts in the launch directory |
| `/fork [prompt]` | A background session with its own agent-view row | Full history, model, permission mode, effort, grants | Nothing on its own; cross-session messages only | The copy creates its own worktree before editing; sessions with a replaced system prompt or a `--tools` allowlist cannot be forked |
| `/subtask <task>`; Agent tool `subagent_type: "fork"` | A background forked subagent | Same system prompt, tools, model and full history; shares the parent's prompt cache | Final result arrives as a message in the main conversation; its tool calls stay out | Forks cannot spawn forks; a panel below the prompt lets you open, steer or stop (`x`) each fork |
| `/rewind` (Esc Esc) | Nothing new; truncates to a checkpoint | n/a | n/a | Restores code, conversation or both; "Summarize up to here" compresses in place; keeps the 100 most recent checkpoints |
| Agent SDK `fork_session` / `forkSession` | A new session ID from `resume` | A copy of the history, not the filesystem | Nothing | Prompt-cache sharing undocumented |

Sources: [Manage sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sessions.md), [Commands](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/commands.md), [Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md), [Agent view](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-view.md), [Interactive mode](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/interactive-mode.md), [Checkpointing](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/checkpointing.md), [Desktop](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/desktop.md), [Worktrees](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/worktrees.md), [Agent SDK sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/sessions.md).

A few semantics matter beyond the table. Fork mode, which lets Claude start forks on its own, has been on by default in interactive terminal sessions since **v2.1.232 (13 August 2026)** and everywhere since **v2.1.302 (16 October 2026)**. Deny `Agent(fork)` or set `CLAUDE_CODE_FORK_SUBAGENT=0` to stop it ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents.md)). The public docs never name the Agent-tool parameter that selects a fork. The Agent tool in a current Claude Code session (v2.1.3xx, November 2026) does accept `subagent_type: "fork"`, and that fork inherits the full conversation and runs in the background, which is what `/subtask` wraps. Treat that as confirmed by the tool itself, not by the docs. With agent view off, `/fork` falls back to a forked subagent and `/subtask` is unavailable ([Commands](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/commands.md)). `/btw` is cheap while the prompt cache is warm, and it replays the 20 most recent side exchanges ([Interactive mode](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/interactive-mode.md)). `/fork` appends its isolation instruction at the end of the copied conversation, so the original's prompt cache stays intact ([Prompt caching in Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching.md)). The vocabulary is also recent and has shifted. `/branch` took over the original `/fork` name in v2.1.77 in March 2026. `/fork` became "copy the session", and the forked-subagent behavior moved to `/subtask`, in v2.1.212 in July 2026 ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md); [Agent view](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-view.md)).

The gaps line up with the human problems above. Nothing pins a question to a passage while Claude streams: a message typed during a turn is queued and delivered within that turn ([Interactive mode](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/interactive-mode.md)). Nothing merges a `/branch` back, and an open feature request asks for exactly that ([issue listing](https://claudeissues.com/issue/69712-feature-conversation-branches-branch-should-surface-in-agent-view-with-a-merge-b)). No documented field records lineage. Transcripts are JSONL files whose "entry format is internal to Claude Code and changes between versions" ([Manage sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sessions.md)). A scan of local transcripts under `~/.claude/projects/` found a `parentSessionId` only in a subagent's `fork-context-ref` record, never on a `/branch` or `/fork` copy. The CLI forks only at the end of a session: `claude --help` offers no flag to fork at an earlier message. The docs imply a workaround, untested: run `/branch`, then `/rewind` inside the branch and choose "Restore conversation." That truncates the copy and leaves the original intact ([Checkpointing](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/checkpointing.md)). The docs also do not say whether a fork made after compaction inherits the compacted summary or the full history. On claude.ai the gap is wider. Editing an earlier message creates a new version of the chat ([Artifacts help](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17153992-what-are-artifacts-and-how-do-i-use-them)), but Anthropic's help center lists "Branching a conversation from an earlier point isn't available" as a current limitation ([Cowork and chat](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/16761823-claude-cowork-and-chat-are-one-claude)).

### What everyone else ships

The market has settled on three patterns. A **fork** is an independent copy that sends nothing back. Examples are ChatGPT's "Branch in new chat" (4 September 2025) ([release notes](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/6825453-chatgpt-release-notes)), Gemini's "Branch in a new chat" (staged rollout from late April 2026) ([Gemini help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13666746)), Raycast's Branch Chat (16 July 2025) ([Raycast](https://www.raycast.com/changelog/1-101-0)), Cursor's Fork Chat (v2.5, February 2026) ([Cursor 2.5](https://cursor.com/changelog/2-5)), VS Code's `/fork` (1.110, March 2026) ([VS Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/updates/v1_110)), and Codex's `/fork` ([Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/codex/reference/slash-commands)). A **side thread** sees the parent, stays out of its history, and can optionally be pulled back: Claude's `/btw`, Codex's `/side`, Cursor's side chats and Open WebUI's `/side` ([Open WebUI](https://docs.openwebui.com/features/chat-conversations/chat-features/)). A **delegate** starts with narrower context and returns a summary or artifacts. Examples are Claude's subagents, Roo Code's Orchestrator, where "only this summary returns to the parent" ([Roo](https://roocodeinc.github.io/Roo-Code/features/boomerang-tasks)), and Devin's managed child sessions ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/devin-can-now-manage-devins)). Only two designs both inherit the parent mid-conversation and bring something back: Claude's `/subtask`, which posts its result automatically, and Cursor's side chats, which the user pulls in with an @-mention.

Several designs are worth copying. Cursor 3.11 (10 July 2026) defines a side chat as "a parallel thread, not a fork." It is durable and stays attached to its parent. It receives the parent's history as hidden reference context, can be opened from a selected passage of the transcript, and returns only when the user @-mentions it ([Cursor help](https://cursor.com/help/ai-features/side-chats)). Codex 0.163.0 (October 2026) keeps side conversations alive as you move between threads, and routes answers to pending prompts back to the thread that asked ([Codex changelog](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/changelog)). Warp opens a fork in a split pane beside the original and adds `/fork-and-compact` and `/fork-from` an earlier query ([Warp](https://docs.warp.dev/agents/local-agents/interacting-with-agents/conversation-forking.md)). Dust seeds a branch with a compacted summary of the parent instead of a transcript copy, marks the parent message, and shows a "Branched from" link ([Dust](https://docs.dust.tt/docs/user-documentation/agents/branch-a-conversation)). LibreChat makes the inheritance question explicit with three fork scopes ([LibreChat](https://www.librechat.ai/docs/features/fork)), and Msty shows a side-by-side diff of two branches ([Msty](https://docs.msty.ai/studio/conversations/branch-explorer)). Microsoft Copilot lets a follow-up work only on a selected passage, without creating a durable child thread ([Copilot, August 2026](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoft-copilot-blog/what%E2%80%99s-new-in-microsoft-copilot--august-2026/4551960)). The sharpest counter-signal comes from Amp, which removed its fork command: "We'd rather spend our time perfecting handoff and thread mentions than support fork." In its place, Amp starts new threads seeded from a stated goal, which pull in other threads' context by reference ([Amp handoff](https://ampcode.com/news/handoff); [fork removal](https://www.havoptic.com/r/amp-stick-a-fork-in-it)).

Across the market, the question "what does a branch inherit?" has at least five shipping answers: a full copy, a copy up to a chosen message, a summary, hidden reference context, or instructions only. Merging barely exists. Canvas tools such as Flowith's "Group Nodes", Canvas Chat's multi-select and TypingMind's "Finalize" combine several branches' context into a new prompt ([Flowith](https://flowith.io/blog/context-playground); [Canvas Chat](https://ericmjl.github.io/blog/2025/12/31/canvas-chat-a-visual-interface-for-thinking-with-llms/); [TypingMind](https://docs.typingmind.com/chat-management/fork-chats)). None of them reconciles two transcripts, and no product applies a human-reviewed conclusion back to the trunk.

## Model the conversation as a question graph over a frozen trunk

An AI that models a two-person conversation can draw on five established kinds of representation. Question-centric models (QUD, KoS) track the open questions. The Grosz-Sidner model tracks an attention stack. Rhetorical-relation models (RST trees, SDRT graphs) label how passages relate. State-centric models track dialogue state and common ground. Thread or segment models split talk into topics and threads. KoS is the one built for two people. Each participant has a private information state plus a shared "dialogue gameboard" that holds agreed facts, pending moves and a ranked list of open questions, headed by the current topic ([KoS formalization](https://hawkrobe.github.io/linglib/docs/Linglib/Theories/Pragmatics/Dialogue/KOS/Basic.html)). That maps neatly onto the user's experience, as a synthesis rather than a finding. The questions a listener catalogs while the other party talks are private entries on their question list that have not yet been made public. Clark and Schaefer model every contribution as a presentation followed by an acceptance ([Clark & Schaefer 1989](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_7)), which describes what a merge should be: the branch presents its answer, and the human accepts it into common ground.

Fine-tuned LLMs now match or beat specialized parsers on the heavier structures. Llamipa parses SDRT incrementally, one unit at a time, reaching **0.775 F1 on predicting links** on the STAC multi-party corpus ([Llamipa](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18256v3)). A fine-tuned Llama 2 70B set the state of the art on RST parsing ([Maekawa et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05065v1)). Fine-tuned models separate interleaved threads in Discord developer chat at **0.90 Shen F**, a standard disentanglement score ([CASCON 2025](https://conf.researchr.org/details/cascon-2025/technical-papers-track/38/Revisiting-Thread-Disentanglement-in-Technical-Chat-with-Fine-Tuned-LLMs-and-Graph-Ba)). Models without fine-tuning are weaker exactly where a live branching UI would lean on them. Out-of-the-box GPT-4o separates threads poorly ([LREC 2026](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-lrec/2026.lrec-main.229/)). GPT-4 wrote fluent questions-under-discussion that failed three checks: whether the text actually answers them, whether they assume only what has already been said, and whether they tie to the right sentence ([QUDeval](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14520)). Spotting that a question asked three turns ago was never answered is not yet a named benchmark task. The closest work detects unanswerable questions in conversational search ([Łajewska & Balog 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.11452)). The design consequence is to let the human create threads by branching, which removes the disentanglement problem, and to show model-proposed open questions only as dimmed suggestions the human confirms.

The discourse-modeling notes propose four layers. A **transcript graph** is the append-only ground truth. Each message points to its parent, which makes branches a tree, and typed cross-reference edges ("answers", "contradicts", "refers to") turn it into the mostly-tree graph that the corpus evidence describes. An **issue graph** makes questions first-class nodes with a status, anchored to spans of text, plus a per-branch stack of what is open now. A **state document**, versioned and owned by the trunk, holds decisions, facts, assumptions and open issues, the equivalent of KoS's agreed facts. An **entity registry** gives each thing mentioned a stable ID across its mentions. The model can feed the first two layers as it writes. Graphologue had GPT-4 tag entities and relationships inline while streaming, parsed the partial stream in real time, and drew placeholder nodes when a reference arrived before its entity. It fixed formatting drift with prompt rules and a second tagging pass ([Graphologue](https://hci.ucsd.edu/papers/graphologue.pdf)). Anthropic's fine-grained tool streaming offers a separate structured channel: it streams a tool call's JSON input as the model generates it, with the caveat that partial or invalid JSON can arrive ([fine-grained tool streaming](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/fine-grained-tool-streaming)). That suggests a two-channel design. Light inline anchors in the prose give each passage a stable ID, and a structured call at the end of each turn lists open questions, conclusions and changed commitments. Heavier parsing runs in the background after the turn ends.

Cost should decide what a branch inherits as much as the user experience does, and the numbers are stark. Anthropic's prompt cache only helps when the start of a request is **100% identical** to an earlier one. Cache reads cost **0.1x** the normal input price (**0.05x** on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5). Writing to the cache costs 1.25x for a 5-minute lifetime or 2x for one hour. Each read resets the clock, and changing thinking or effort settings invalidates the cached messages ([Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). That favors a **frozen trunk prefix plus a branch preamble**. The branch resends the trunk's messages up to the fork point byte-for-byte, then adds one new block. That block quotes the anchored passage, states the question, sets a scope ("answer this; do not advance the main task; end with a conclusion"), and names the state-document version. The branch sees only its own line back to the root, not later trunk turns, sibling branches or side questions. Isolation has evidence behind it. Across 30 software scenarios, ContextBranch's branched conversations used **58.1% less context** (31.0 to 13.0 messages) and scored 2.5% higher on quality (Cohen's d ≈ 0.73) ([ContextBranch](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13914)). Seeding a branch with a summary, as Dust and Amp do, pays for a fresh cache write and loses detail. It wins only when the trunk is huge and the question narrow, and no study compares the policies head-to-head ([Conversation Tree Architecture](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21278v1)). One cache detail matters for branches opened later. A branch opened 30 minutes after its question was parked usually pays once to rewrite the cache up to the fork point, because that entry has expired, and then reads from cache. Every branch should show a **context manifest**, for example: "knows: trunk through turn 14, state doc v6; does not know: turns 15-22, branches B2 and B4." That directly answers CanvasConvo's "I didn't know what the branch knows."

Merging back has well-tested precedents outside AI.

| Precedent | Lesson for bringing a branch home |
|---|---|
| [`git merge`](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-merge) | Never merge a whole branch transcript into the trunk; it brings back the clutter that branching removed |
| `git merge --squash` | Default: one compact confluence message at the end of the trunk |
| `git cherry-pick`; ContextBranch's selective injection | Advanced: carry back selected claims, checked against the trunk's commitments |
| `git rebase` | If the trunk has moved since the fork, re-check the conclusion against the newer trunk first |
| [Zulip resolve](https://zulip.com/help/resolve-a-topic); [Google Docs resolve](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/65129) | Resolving is a status change; the branch stays retrievable and can be reopened |
| [Slack "also send to channel"](https://slack.com/help/articles/115000769927-Use-threads-to-organize-discussions) | The human chooses what crosses into the main flow |
| Peer-review response letters | Key the confluence by question: quote it, answer it, and say where the state document changed |
| [Wikum recursive summaries](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~axz/papers/wikum.pdf) | A collapsed branch shows a summary that expands into sub-summaries, then the transcript |

The recommended confluence combines three things. The anchored question gets a resolved record. One confluence message goes at the end of the trunk, for example: "Q3, anchored at turn 14, resolved: … State changes: … Based on trunk at turn 14." The model proposes a diff to the state document, and the human approves it. Appending at the end keeps the trunk's cached prefix intact, whereas ContextBranch-style insertion mid-history would invalidate the cache from that point on. If the conclusion contradicts a commitment made after the fork, it is marked stale and offered for re-checking against the current trunk. `/subtask` already does half of this by posting a fork's result into the parent, but it posts automatically. The acceptance model above argues for a proposal the user approves. No user study compares merge styles.

The resulting interaction loop has six steps. **Pin** a question to a passage while the model streams, without interrupting it. Optionally **peek** with a `/btw`-style answer that is not saved. **Surface** parked questions only at the end of the turn, because interruptions at coarse task breakpoints cost the least ([Iqbal & Bailey 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-TOCHI10.pdf)). **Open** a branch when the user chooses to. **Conclude**, and review the confluence point by point. **Return** to the end of the trunk, where a one-line card expands Wikum-style. Escalation runs from a quick side question, to a parked question, to a branch. The main surface stays linear chat, with a narrow outline sidebar and breadcrumbs, as in Branchat; the canvas is an optional overview. Depth is capped at two, matching CanvasConvo's observed maximum and Claude Code's rule that a fork cannot fork. The inbox limits how many questions can be open and reviews stale ones at session end. Both guard against hypertext's twin problems of disorientation and cognitive overhead ([Conklin, via "Lost in hyperspace"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_in_hyperspace)). Light tagging during the conversation has precedent: Tilda let chat participants tag and summarize while talking, and people catching up later "felt less hurried" ([Tilda](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~axz/papers/cscw_tilda.pdf)). A trimmed data model follows, adapted from the discourse-modeling notes, with session and terminal fields added for Herdr.

```ts
interface Question { id: string; anchor: { sessionId: string; messageUuid: string; quote?: string };
  text: string; origin: 'human' | 'model_suggested'; createdAtTurn: number; branchId?: string;
  status: 'parked' | 'peeked' | 'active' | 'resolved' | 'discarded' | 'reopened' }
interface Branch { id: string; sessionId: string; parentSessionId: string; questionId?: string;
  forkPoint: { messageUuid: string; turn: number }; contextPolicy: 'frozen_prefix' | 'summary_seeded';
  depth: 0 | 1 | 2; mode: 'lazy' | 'background'; label: string; terminal?: string;
  status: 'running' | 'awaiting_human' | 'concluded' | 'merged' | 'discarded' | 'stale' }
interface Confluence { branchId: string; questionId: string; answer: string; basedOnTrunkTurn: number;
  mergeMode: 'squash' | 'cherry_pick' | 'note_only' | 'discard' | 'defer';
  stateDiff: { op: 'add' | 'update' | 'retract'; text: string }[];
  status: 'proposed' | 'accepted' | 'edited' | 'rejected' }
```

## Herdr already holds the tree, launcher and panel, minus session ids

Herdr 0.1.12 describes its window as a tree. Layout files nest `row`, `column` and `stack` containers to any depth, and the leaf blocks show a mod, a terminal, the active tab's panes, or the focused agent ([AGENTS.md](~/.config/herdr-app/AGENTS.md)). The personal `custom` layout already contains a hidden right-hand `panel` column (368 px, resizable from 280 to 900 px). It holds a `panel-body` stack of Files, Changes and Quota mods, which the `my-tabs` mod shows and hides ([custom.json](~/.config/herdr-app/layouts/custom.json); [my-tabs](~/.config/herdr-app/mods/my-tabs/main.js)). A Branches mod can be a fourth child of that stack. Because a stack shows one child at a time, it can instead get its own column if it should not compete with the others. Every pane draws a header mod that knows its own pane and terminal, so a cloned header can carry a branch button beside split-right ([pane mod](~/.config/herdr-app/mods/pane/main.js)). Overlays can host a mod or a whole layout, including a live terminal block, which suits a branch preview.

Launching processes is a built-in capability. `pane.split`, `tab.new`, `space.new` and `terminal.open` all accept an `argv`, so `claude --resume <id> --fork-session -n <label> "<preamble>"` can open beside its parent. `pane.split` has no `task` field, so the prompt goes in as Claude's positional argument ([AGENTS.md](~/.config/herdr-app/AGENTS.md)). Launching with `hc run --parent <t> --outlive-parent --no-notify --label branch.parent=<sid>` registers the fork as a child terminal. The shipped `agents` mod already draws terminals as a recursive parent-child tree with connector lines and folding. In that tree, clicking a child peeks at it, "keep" promotes a child into a pane, and "Send back to <parent>" demotes it again ([agents mod](~/.config/herdr-app/mods/agents/main.js)). State can live in `shared()` keys, which persist to `shared.json` and re-render every mod when set. `watchFolder` reacts to transcript changes without timers, and `keys.json` can route a shortcut to a mod ([herdr.d.ts](~/.config/herdr-app/herdr.d.ts)). Two constraints shape the design. A `<mod>.<command>` shortcut reaches only mod copies currently on screen, so a key that must work while the panel is hidden should use `mod.open:<mod>`, the pattern the Linear mod uses on Cmd-L. And there is no tree or graph widget. Lists, accordions and absolute positioning exist, and vector drawing with `PathBuilder` is proven for fills, while stroked lines appear in the type definitions but no installed mod uses them ([gpui-kit.d.ts](~/.config/herdr-app/gpui-kit.d.ts)).

The weak link is identity. Herdr's agent and pane records carry status, app, title and parent terminal, but **no Claude session id or transcript path** ([herdr.d.ts](~/.config/herdr-app/herdr.d.ts)). The research traced a chain to recover one: from the focused terminal, to its foreground process id, to Claude Code's `~/.claude/sessions/<pid>.json`, which maps a process id to its `sessionId`. Each link was observed separately, but the whole chain is unconfirmed, and the sessions file is undocumented internal state. The hook Herdr installed at `~/.claude/hooks/herdr-agent-state.sh` forwards only pane state and drops the hook input's `session_id` and `transcript_path`. It is also inert in this environment because `HERDR_ENV` is unset ([hook script](~/.claude/hooks/herdr-agent-state.sh)). A sturdier route is a hook you own on the `UserPromptSubmit` and `Stop` events, both already wired in `~/.claude/settings.json`. It would write the Herdr terminal id, session id, transcript path and turn number to a file under Herdr's config folder. This is inferred, not tested. `HC_TERMINAL_ID` is present in Claude's environment inside Herdr, and the `Stop` event doubles as the end-of-turn moment when parked questions should surface.

The Herdr notes sketch three designs that share one launch recipe. **Option A** launches branches as `hc` sub-agents, so the shipped agents sidebar becomes the branch tree with almost no UI code. Its limits are real. Lineage is tied to the terminal, not the session, and finished branches disappear because the tree shows only live children. Closing the source kills its branches unless `--outlive-parent` is set. Without `--no-notify`, hc types notes into the parent Claude session's input. **Option B** adds a Branches panel whose lineage is stored per session, so it survives terminal exits. It also adds a launch button in the pane header, previews rendered from the transcript's last assistant message, and "reopen" for closed branches. **Option C** is a floating branch-map window that draws the lineage graph with hand-built layout and curved edges: the most faithful picture, and clearly the most work. The notes recommend A, then B, then C as a view over the same lineage store. One inference turns A's notification hazard into an asset. hc's note channel, which types a child's report into its parent agent's input, is a built-in return path for confluence. It is safe if the mod delivers only when the trunk is idle and only after the human approves the text. Two semantic limits apply to all three options. `--fork-session` copies conversation history, not files, so a branch that edits files edits the same working tree ([Agent SDK sessions](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/sessions.md)). Whether `--resume` finds a session started in a different folder is unconfirmed. None of the recipes was run during the research. Herdr's manual requires a passing `herdr-app check` plus an in-app trial before any mod counts as done ([AGENTS.md](~/.config/herdr-app/AGENTS.md)).

## Conclusion

The market has largely solved the fork and ignored the moment before it. Every serious product now copies a conversation into a new thread, and Claude Code offers more variants than anyone. But nobody captures the question at the instant it forms, while the model is still talking, holds it outside the transcript, and raises it at the end of the turn. That gap sits where the cognitive evidence is strongest (deferred intentions fade within seconds, and readers form a question every couple of sentences) and where the interface literature has no studies at all. A question inbox is therefore both the most novel and the most defensible thing to build. The second takeaway concerns the two choices users actually feel: what a branch knows, and how its answer returns. Cache economics, the isolation research and the river metaphor all point to the same answer: freeze the trunk prefix, show the context manifest, and return the branch's result rather than its transcript, through one approved message at the end of the trunk.

The evidence does not yet justify a canvas-first design, or a claim that branching lowers cognitive load. It justifies a chat-first tool that makes non-linearity cheap and visible, capped at two levels. The literature also lacks longitudinal data, so the lineage store can double as an instrument. Logging questions parked, branches opened, depth, resolution rate and abandoned questions during daily work would produce the multi-week, real-work record that no published study has.

## Build order: resolver and launcher first, inbox second, canvas last

The order below tests the unconfirmed links (session resolution and the fork launch) first, while failure is cheap, then adds the piece no product ships.

| Priority | Build | Herdr pieces it uses | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Session resolver and lineage store.** A Claude Code hook (`UserPromptSubmit`, `Stop`) writes `HC_TERMINAL_ID → session_id, transcript_path, turn` to a file. A mod mirrors it and keeps `shared("branches")` as child → parent session, fork turn, label, question and terminal. | `watchFolder`, `shared()`, `terminals()`; fallback through the pid chain and `~/.claude/sessions/<pid>.json` | Focusing any Claude pane yields its session id without the pid chain |
| 2 | **Branch launcher (Option A).** A `mod.open:branch` key or a branch button in a cloned `my-pane` header opens a one-line input, then runs `hc run --parent <t> --outlive-parent --no-notify --label branch.parent=<sid> -- claude --resume <sid> --fork-session -n "<label>" "<preamble>"`. Optionally add `--session-id <uuid>` to record lineage before launch (untested combination). | Overlay dialog with `Input`, `hc`, the shipped `agents` tree, peek and keep | The fork appears nested under its source in the agents sidebar; `herdr-app check` passes |
| 3 | **Question inbox.** A global `mod.open:inbox` key opens the input even while the agent is working. The question is stored as `parked` with `{sessionId, last assistant message uuid, optional quote}`. A count chip in the pane header expands only when the `Stop` hook fires. Actions: peek (type `/btw`), branch (priority 2), discard. | Overlay dialog, pane header mod, `shared("questions")` | Parking costs one keystroke and never interrupts the stream; stale questions surface at session end |
| 4 | **Branches panel with context manifest.** A `branches` block in `panel-body` (or its own column) shows the focused session's ancestors, siblings and children, with live status, the last assistant message as markdown, "knows / does not know" lines, and "reopen" for closed branches. | `panel-body` stack, `TextView`, `agents()` status, transcript tail through `exec` beyond the 1 MiB `readTextFile` limit | Lineage survives closed terminals; every branch states what it inherited |
| 5 | **Rejoin (confluence).** On a concluded branch, ask it for a conclusion block and let the human edit it. When the trunk is idle, deliver one confluence message to the end of the trunk (via an hc note or typed input) and mark the question resolved. Optionally append the decision to a per-session state file. | Agent status changes, hc note channel, `shared()` | The trunk grows by one message per resolved branch; nothing arrives mid-turn |
| Later | **Branch-map window (Option C)** and model-emitted anchors | Overlay `window`, `PathBuilder` | Only after weeks of lineage data show the tree has outgrown a list |

Avoid two things. First, automatic branching or merging. Model-suggested questions should stay dimmed suggestions, and `/subtask`-style automatic posting skips the human acceptance step. Second, nesting deeper than two levels. Keep one semantic limit in mind: the launcher forks at the end of the session, so a question parked at turn 14 and opened at turn 22 also inherits turns 15-22. The preamble should quote the anchor and say so, and the manifest should show it. If you share the mod, Herdr's community rules require a `RECIPE.md` and a disclosure that it runs `exec` and `hc` and reads `~/.claude` ([AGENTS.md](~/.config/herdr-app/AGENTS.md)).
