## 1. Discourse structure: which theories model conversation as a stack or tree, and what empirical support exists?

### Takeaway
Nearly every major formal model of discourse is hierarchical. The list includes Grosz & Sidner's focus stack, Roberts's QUD stack, QUD trees, RST trees, SDRT's right frontier, Clark & Schaefer's nested contributions, and conversation analysis's nested side and insertion sequences. So "conversation has stacked or tree-like structure, not a flat line" is mainstream theory, not a fringe claim. The empirical picture is "mostly hierarchical, with real exceptions." Stack-like attachment constraints hold about 95% of the time in annotated corpora. But crossed dependencies and nodes with several parents make strict trees inadequate. The safest description is a mostly-tree-shaped graph. This is evidence about how discourse is *structured*. It is not direct evidence that people mentally *represent* a branching tree.

### Cited Findings

**Stack models**
- Grosz & Sidner split discourse structure into three components. *Linguistic structure* is the segments. *Intentional structure* is the discourse-segment purposes, related by dominance and satisfaction-precedence. *Attentional state* is modeled as a **focus stack**: a focus space is pushed when a segment opens and popped when its purpose is satisfied. Interruptions and digressions are pushes, and cue phrases such as "but anyway" signal a pop back to an earlier space. [PR][B] — [Grosz & Sidner 1986, "Attention, Intentions, and the Structure of Discourse," *Computational Linguistics* 12(3):175–204](https://aclanthology.org/J86-3001/)
- Roberts puts a **QUD stack** at the center of discourse. The bottom of the stack is the overarching "superquestion." Once a question is accepted onto the stack, participants "will be committed to answering it until it is completely answered or until it is determined to be unanswerable." Moves must be relevant to the immediate (top) QUD, and irrelevant moves are "non-sequiturs." [PR][V via survey] — [Roberts 2012 (orig. 1996), "Information structure in discourse," *Semantics & Pragmatics* 5(6), doi:10.3765/sp.5.6](https://doi.org/10.3765/sp.5.6); summarized in [Fu 2025, "A Survey of QUD Models for Discourse Processing," arXiv:2502.15573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15573) [PP]
- Ginzburg's dialogue gameboard (KoS) tracks shared commitments, the latest move, and QUD for each participant. QUD there is a **partially ordered set** with a maximal element (MaxQUD), not a strict stack, so several questions can be open at once. [PR book][B] — [Ginzburg 2012, *The Interactive Stance: Meaning for Conversation*, Oxford University Press](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199697922.001.0001)

**Tree and graph models**
- QUD-tree annotation (Riester 2019; De Kuthy et al. 2018) arranges implicit questions and subquestions into trees. Its "Back-to-the-Roots" constraint adopts SDRT's right frontier: an incoming unit attaches "as low as necessary to allow for anaphoric reference but as high as possible to facilitate the conclusion of the current lower-level discourse and return to the main question of the discourse." That is an explicit "return pop" toward the root. [V] — [Fu 2025 survey, arXiv:2502.15573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15573) [PP]
- Büring's "d-trees" model discourse as a tree of questions and subquestions, which licenses contrastive-topic intonation. [PR][B] — [Büring 2003, "On D-Trees, Beans, and B-Accents," *Linguistics & Philosophy* 26(5):511–545](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1025887707652)
- Rhetorical Structure Theory represents *monologic* text as a tree of nucleus–satellite relations over adjacent spans. [PR][B] — [Mann & Thompson 1988, "Rhetorical Structure Theory," *Text* 8(3):243–281](https://doi.org/10.1515/text.1.1988.8.3.243)
- SDRT, which was designed with dialogue in mind, uses graphs whose relations are either coordinating or subordinating. The **Right Frontier Constraint** limits new attachments to the last unit or the nodes that dominate it, which is a stack-like accessibility constraint. [PR book][B] — [Asher & Lascarides 2003, *Logics of Conversation*, Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/logics-of-conversation/)
- Clark & Schaefer model every contribution as a *presentation phase* plus an *acceptance phase*. An acceptance can itself be a contribution, so contributions nest recursively into hierarchical structures. [PR][B] — [Clark & Schaefer 1989, "Contributing to Discourse," *Cognitive Science* 13(2):259–294](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_7)

**Conversation analysis: nesting in real talk**
- Jefferson's "side sequences" are excursions from an ongoing sequence, for example a misapprehension followed by a clarification, after which the talk returns to the main line. [PR chapter][B] — Jefferson, G. 1972, "Side Sequences," in D. Sudnow (ed.), *Studies in Social Interaction*, Free Press, pp. 294–338 (no stable URL; standard citation)
- Schegloff's insertion sequences and later "sequence organization" describe pre-expansions, insert expansions (between the first and second part of a base adjacency pair), and post-expansions. Inserts can themselves contain inserts. [PR book][B] — [Schegloff 2007, *Sequence Organization in Interaction: A Primer in Conversation Analysis*, Vol. 1, Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511791208); Schegloff 1972, "Notes on a conversational practice: Formulating place," in Sudnow (ed.), *Studies in Social Interaction*.
- Levinson argues that "much deeper center-embedding can be found in dialogue or conversation structure than can be found in syntax." In a 2011 conference version he writes that clausal center-embedding is effectively capped at two deep in spoken languages, while interactive discourse breaks this barrier "routinely." He speculates that the **action-planning system's ability to hold a stack of subgoals** underlies recursion in pragmatics. [PR][V via abstract and search summaries] — [Levinson 2013, "Recursion in Pragmatics," *Language* 89(1):149–162, doi:10.1353/lan.2013.0005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/recursion-in-pragmatics/89BE56395B7811B32DAD3F830AE9D390)
  - Contested: Legate, Pesetsky & Yang reply that his claims are "at best unwarranted." [PR][V] — [Legate, Pesetsky & Yang 2014, "Recursive misrepresentations: A reply to Levinson (2013)," *Language* 90(2)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/recursive-misrepresentations-a-reply-to-levinson-2013/234B497EB948723F05548AFEC6418678)

**Empirical tests of the structure**
- **Stack-like constraint mostly holds.** About 100 documents were each annotated twice by five non-expert annotators, and SDRT's Right Frontier Constraint was "respected about 95% of the time." [PR][V] — [Afantenos & Asher 2010, "Testing SDRT's Right Frontier," COLING 2010, arXiv:1006.5880](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.5880)
- **Strict trees are inadequate.** In 135 double-annotated newspaper texts, Wolf & Gibson found many crossed dependencies and many nodes with multiple parents. They propose chain graphs instead of trees. As reported by Egg & Redeker, about 12.5% of relations would have to be removed to eliminate all crossings, and elaboration relations account for 50.5% of crossed dependencies. [PR][V] — [Wolf & Gibson 2005, "Representing Discourse Coherence: A Corpus-Based Study," *Computational Linguistics* 31(2):249–287](https://doi.org/10.1162/0891201054223977); figures via [Egg & Redeker 2010, "How Complex is Discourse Structure?" LREC](https://preview.aclanthology.org/naacl24-info/L10-1547)
  - Counterpoint: Egg & Redeker argue that several configurations which violate tree constraints can still be analyzed as trees. A review also notes that Wolf & Gibson's annotation protocol *allowed* crossings and re-entrancy, which may inflate them. [V] — [Egg & Redeker 2010](https://preview.aclanthology.org/naacl24-info/L10-1547)
- **QUD trees are only moderately reliable to annotate.** Two trained annotators building QUD trees for an English interview transcript reached Cohen's κ = 0.52 ("moderate agreement"). [V] — De Kuthy, Reiter & Riester 2018 (LREC), as reported in [Fu 2025 survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15573)
- **Comprehenders continuously generate questions that are often left open.** TED-Q had crowdworkers read TED-talk transcripts incrementally, entering at each probe point (every two sentences) a question the text evoked. They then rated whether earlier questions were answered in the next two chunks. The study produced 2,412 evoked questions. Mean answeredness was 2.50 on a 1–5 scale, "so questions tend towards remaining unanswered." "Almost half" (1,107) were at least partially answered and 367 were completely answered. The authors read this as evidence that "participants ask questions that anticipate speakers' upcoming discourse moves." A pilot found that questions not answered by the first probe point were not answered at the next two either. [PR][V] — [Westera, Mayol & Rohde 2020, "TED-Q: TED Talks and the Questions they Evoke," LREC 2020, pp. 1118–1127](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.141/)
- Kehler & Rohde report experimental (psycholinguistic) support for an expectation-driven QUD model of interpretation. [PR][B] — [Kehler & Rohde 2017, *Discourse Processes* 54(3):219–238](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2016.1169069)
- Cue phrases such as "now," "anyway," and "well" have discourse uses that mark structural boundaries, including returns, and can be told apart from their sentential uses by prosody and text features. [PR][B] — [Hirschberg & Litman 1993, "Empirical Studies on the Disambiguation of Cue Phrases," *Computational Linguistics* 19(3):501–530](https://aclanthology.org/J93-3003/)
- **Text chat specifically.** Multi-party chat channels interleave several simultaneous conversations, which have to be "disentangled" into reply-to threads. The annotations form a reply graph, not a single line. [PR][B] — [Elsner & Charniak 2008, "You Talking to Me?," ACL](https://aclanthology.org/P08-1095/); [Kummerfeld et al. 2019, "A Large-Scale Corpus for Conversation Disentanglement," ACL](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1374/)
- **Grounding depends on medium constraints.** Clark & Brennan list eight constraints that shape grounding: copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, and revisability. Text media add reviewability and revisability, which speech lacks. [PR chapter][B] — [Clark & Brennan 1991, "Grounding in Communication," in *Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition*, APA, pp. 127–149](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006)

### Inferences
- Theory is close to unanimous that conversation has hierarchical *structure*, with returns ("pops") to earlier open questions. A flat transcript is a *serialization* of that structure, not the structure itself.
- Two notions should be kept apart. The **stack** is attentional: which questions are currently open and accessible (Grosz & Sidner, Roberts). The **tree or graph** is the record of everything opened, answered, or abandoned (QUD trees, SDRT). A linear chat UI makes neither explicit. It shows the serialized log, so the user has to keep the stack in their head.
- Wolf & Gibson's crossings and Ginzburg's partially ordered QUD suggest a report should not claim conversation is a *strict* tree. "Mostly tree-shaped with cross-links" is the defensible version. That matters for design, because pure tree UIs would need cross-links or merges.
- Per Clark & Brennan, text chat already provides reviewability that speech lacks. So the argument against linear chat should be about *structure and navigation* (finding and returning to open questions), not about memory capacity alone.
- TED-Q is the closest empirical analogue to "parallel cataloguing of follow-ups": readers generate a question roughly every two sentences, and more than half stay at least partly unanswered locally.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures how often natural conversations return to an earlier open question after a digression (a "pop rate"), or how deep nesting typically gets in everyday talk. Levinson's specific embedding-depth examples could not be checked because the full-text link returned 404.
- Most corpus evidence (RST, Wolf & Gibson, right-frontier tests) comes from *written monologue*, not dialogue. I did not find right-frontier violation statistics for the multi-party-chat STAC corpus.
- The theoretical evidence is structural and analytic. It shows that conversations *can be described* as stacks or trees. It does not show that speakers maintain an explicit mental tree.
- The term "return pop" is informal shorthand built on Grosz & Sidner's push/pop vocabulary. It is not an established term in conversation analysis, which speaks of "resumption" or "return."

## 2. Cognitive limits: holding pending questions and intentions while listening

### Takeaway
There is no experimental literature on "forgetting your question while the other person keeps talking" as such. There is strong adjacent evidence. Working-memory focus holds about 4 chunks. Deferred intentions are forgotten fast, within 5–40 seconds, especially under load or interruption. Even about 3-second interruptions double sequence errors. And conversational memory is very poor overall. Together these make it a well-grounded inference that a listener holding several follow-up questions through a long turn will lose some of them. The "tip of the tongue" framing is technically the wrong construct. TOT is word-retrieval failure. Losing a question is a prospective-memory and goal-decay failure.

### Cited Findings
- **Capacity.** The focus of attention holds about 4 chunks (range roughly 3–5), revising Miller's "7±2." [PR][B] — [Cowan 2001, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1):87–114](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922); [Miller 1956, "The magical number seven, plus or minus two," *Psychological Review* 63(2):81–97](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158)
- **Deferred intentions are forgotten quickly.** In three experiments participants had to withhold a response until a task change that came 5, 15, or 40 seconds later. Forgetting was rapid under demanding conditions and when the delay was interrupted, "at levels that would be considered significant in applied settings." Strategic rehearsal and implementation intentions did not prevent it. The authors conclude that maintaining intentions over brief delays "is not a trivial task for the human cognitive system." [PR][V] — [Einstein, McDaniel, Williford, Pagan & Dismukes 2003, "Forgetting of intentions in demanding situations is rapid," *J. Experimental Psychology: Applied* 9(3):147–162](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Einstein-JEPA03.pdf)
- **Goals decay and must be refreshed.** In the "memory for goals" model, suspended goals lose activation over time and need rehearsal or environmental cues to be resumed. This is the basis of the "resumption lag" after interruptions. [PR][B] — [Altmann & Trafton 2002, "Memory for goals: an activation-based model," *Cognitive Science* 26(1):39–83](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2)
- **Brief interruptions derail the thread.** Interruptions averaging about 2.8 seconds doubled the rate of sequence (place-keeping) errors, and 4.4-second interruptions tripled it. Non-sequence errors were unaffected, which suggests that the damage is specifically to keeping one's place and not to attention generally. [PR][V] — [Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick 2014, "Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought," *J. Experimental Psychology: General* 143(1):215–226](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986); press summary: [MSU Today 2013](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2013/brief-interruptions-spawn-errors)
- **Interrupted knowledge work** is completed faster but with more stress, frustration, and effort. [PR][B] — [Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008, "The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress," CHI 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072)
- **Planning a reply costs comprehension.** An EEG study replicated early response planning during an incoming question and concluded that "early response planning can indeed impact comprehension processing" of the ongoing turn. Fast responders showed reduced anticipatory processing of the question. [PR][V] — [Bögels, Casillas & Levinson 2018, "Planning versus comprehension in turn-taking," *Neuropsychologia*](https://chatterlab.uchicago.edu/lab-publications/Bogels_et_al_2018_Planning_versus_comprehension_in_turn_taking_Neuropsychologia.pdf)
- **Memory for conversation is poor.** Participants reportedly recalled only about 10% of their conversations immediately afterward, falling to about 4% a month later. *Caveat:* the search snippet giving these figures could not be confirmed as belonging to the 1984 paper rather than the 1987 follow-up, so attribute cautiously. [PR][V-partial] — [Stafford 1984, "Conversational Memory," *Human Communication Research* 10(3):379–402](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.1984.tb00024.x); see also Stafford, Burggraf & Sharkey 1987, *Human Communication Research* 14(2):203–229
- **Questions are generated continuously during comprehension.** TED-Q readers produced a question at every two-sentence probe, and most remained at least partly unanswered over the next four sentences (see Section 1). [PR][V] — [Westera et al. 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.141/)
- **Tip of the tongue is a lexical phenomenon.** TOT is a temporary failure to retrieve a known word, with partial access to its form. It is not loss of an intended question. [PR][B] — [Brown & McNeill 1966, "The 'tip of the tongue' phenomenon," *J. Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 5(4):325–337](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(66)80040-3)
- **Note-taking while listening is itself effortful.** Measured by secondary-task reaction times, its effort is above reading or learning and comparable to text composition. So externalizing questions has a cost too. [PR][B] — [Piolat, Olive & Kellogg 2005, "Cognitive effort during note taking," *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 19(3):291–312](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1086)
  - The popular claim that longhand notes beat laptop notes did not replicate robustly. [PR][B] — [Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014, *Psychological Science* 25(6)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524581); [Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson 2019, *Educational Psychology Review* 31:753–780](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2)

### Inferences
- The interruption-versus-deferral tradeoff is well supported by analogy. **Interrupting** the speaker imposes Altmann-style place-keeping costs on them. **Deferring** imposes Einstein-style intention-forgetting costs on the listener. In human talk, insertion and side sequences are the culturally evolved compromise: a short, bounded interruption with a conventional return.
- In LLM chat the "speaker" (the model) has no place-keeping cost. The interruption-versus-deferral dilemma is therefore *one-sided*: the user can always "interrupt" by forking. A UI that makes forking and returning cheap removes the deferral cost without creating an interruption cost. This is the strongest cognitive-science argument for branching chat.
- A long model response works like a long incoming turn during which the user generates several questions, per TED-Q. With roughly 4 focus slots, demanding comprehension competing for them, and rapid forgetting of deferred intentions, users should be expected to lose follow-ups. Externalizing them (a question queue, or branch stubs) is the standard remedy, but note-taking itself costs effort, so the externalization has to be cheap.

### Gaps
- I found no direct experiment in which listeners hold self-generated questions while a speaker continues and recall is measured. The claim is an inference from prospective-memory, interruption, and comprehension research.
- I found no study that quantifies how many follow-up questions listeners generate per minute of speech. TED-Q's elicitation is forced at fixed probe points, so it gives a lower bound on willingness, not a natural rate.
- Cowan's 4-chunk limit concerns information in the focus of attention generally. No study maps it onto "number of pending questions."

## 3. Dual-task and turn-taking: listeners plan responses while listening

### Takeaway
Cross-linguistic data show that turn transitions happen in about 200 ms, while producing even a single word takes about 600 ms. So listeners must, at least often, plan their reply *while still comprehending*. Levinson calls conversation "constant double tasking." EEG evidence supports early planning, but dual-task evidence suggests later planning, and early planning measurably costs comprehension. The timing pressure that forces this belongs to *speech*. Asynchronous text chat removes it, which weakens a direct analogy to LLM chat.

### Cited Findings
- Across 10 languages, responses to yes/no questions show a universal tendency to minimize gaps and avoid overlap. Mean offsets vary by language from about 7 ms (Japanese) to about 470 ms (Danish). [PR][B] — [Stivers et al. 2009, "Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation," *PNAS* 106(26):10587–10592](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903616106)
- Gaps between turns are "of the order of 200 ms," while "the language production system has latencies of around 600 ms and up for encoding a new word." Naming two nouns takes 740–800 ms, three-word utterances about 900 ms, and a short sentence describing a scene about 1500 ms before speech begins. Mean turn length is 1680 ms (median 1227 ms). Therefore participants "are already encoding their responses while the incoming turn" is still unfolding, and "conversation involves constant double tasking, and this double tasking uses the same language system." [PR][V] — [Levinson & Torreira 2015, "Timing in turn-taking and its implications for processing models of language," *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:731](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731)
- A synthesis argues that turn-taking is a phylogenetically and ontogenetically early system that forces predictive comprehension and early production planning. [PR][B] — [Levinson 2016, "Turn-taking in human communication – origins and implications for language processing," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(1):6–14](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2015.10.010)
- **Early planning (EEG).** When the answer to a question became clear midway through it, listeners showed a large positive ERP effect and an alpha/beta power reduction at that point, consistent with starting to plan "as soon as they have enough information." Mean response latency in the early-cue condition was 640 ms. [PR][V via secondary summary] — [Bögels, Magyari & Levinson 2015, "Neural signatures of response planning occur midway through an incoming question in conversation," *Scientific Reports* 5:12881](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep12881)
- **Counter-evidence (late planning).** From eye movements and dual-task finger-tapping performance, Sjerps & Meyer concluded that speech planning began only near the end of the incoming turn, because planning and comprehension compete for resources. [PR][V via secondary summary] — [Sjerps & Meyer 2015, "Variation in dual-task performance reveals late initiation of speech planning in turn-taking," *Cognition* 136:304–324](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.10.008); characterization per [Frontiers in Psychology 2020 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.593671/pdf)
- **Planning costs comprehension.** See Bögels, Casillas & Levinson 2018 in Section 2. [PR][V] — [Neuropsychologia 2018](https://chatterlab.uchicago.edu/lab-publications/Bogels_et_al_2018_Planning_versus_comprehension_in_turn_taking_Neuropsychologia.pdf)
- **Content versus timing.** Listeners use predictions of content to decide *what* to say early, but not *when* to say it. [PR][B] — [Corps, Crossley, Gambi & Pickering 2018, "Early preparation during turn-taking," *Cognition* 175:77–95](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.01.015). A later paper argues that inter-speaker gaps are a less informative measure of planning than assumed. [PR][B] — [Corps, Knudsen & Meyer 2022, "Overrated gaps," *Cognition* 223:105037](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2022.105037)

### Inferences
- The robust takeaway is that *listening is already a dual task*: comprehending while preparing a next move. Holding *additional* deferred questions beyond the next turn adds load on top of that. This is a reasonable extension, but it is an extension.
- LLM chat inverts the human turn economy. Model "turns" are often hundreds of words, far longer than the 1–2-second human median, and the user cannot take a turn mid-response except by stopping generation. Several questions therefore build up within one turn, and only one can be asked next in a linear thread.
- **Honesty point.** Text chat is asynchronous. The user can pause, re-read, and type at leisure, so the 200 ms timing pressure that drives speech-planning overlap is absent. The analogy holds for *ordering and holding* questions, not for *timing* stress.

### Gaps
- The early-versus-late planning debate is unresolved (Bögels et al. versus Sjerps & Meyer). The report should not say that listeners always plan midway.
- I found no turn-taking or planning research on reading-paced, text-based dialogue with an AI.

## 4. LLM-specific evidence (2023–2026): cognitive load, sensemaking, follow-up management, branching and canvas UIs

### Takeaway
A fast-growing HCI literature (2023–2026) consistently reports the same problems with linear LLM chat. Useful content gets buried in scroll history. Users want to fork and explore alternatives but improvise with tabs and copy-paste. Long or dense responses correlate with more confusion. Prototypes with trees, canvases, and hierarchies help users explore more topics and organize knowledge. **But the evidence is thin.** Most studies are small (N = 8–24), many are preprints or extended abstracts, and cognitive-load benefits are mostly self-reported or null. One controlled sensemaking study found *no* cognitive-load difference against linear chat, and a field study found people still used linear chat as the main surface, using the canvas only selectively. I found no published analysis of how people actually use edit/regenerate branching in ChatGPT or Claude.

### Cited Findings

**Branching, tree, and canvas chat UIs**
- **CanvasConvo / "Conversations in Space"** (2026) pairs chat with a synchronized canvas.
  - **Formative study (N = 8 expert users):**
    - *Context fragmentation:* useful outputs were buried in scroll history, and 6/8 kept separate documents to preserve them.
    - *Forking problem:* 7/8 struggled to explore alternatives without copy-pasting context or cluttering one chat. Workarounds included 15–20 open tabs and typed "BRANCH:" markers.
  - **Five-day field study (N = 24):** participants described long single threads as "hard to manage and easy to get lost in."
  - **Use and context:** branching was used for parallel exploration and what-if variations. A recurring problem was **uncertainty about what context a branch inherited**.
  - **Adoption was uneven:** chat remained the primary surface, and the canvas was used selectively for overview and revisiting.
  - **Metrics:** SUS 78.9; NASA-TLX mean 2.79 (low). Telemetry: 165 conversations, 1,225 messages, 79 branches, 881 canvas zoom actions; chat accounted for 47.2% of logged events and the canvas for 28.3%.
  - **Prior work:** it cites *no* prior empirical study of edit/regenerate branching in ChatGPT or Claude.
  - [PP][V] — [Amin, Adatepe, Fernandes, Buschek & Butz 2026, arXiv:2605.15848v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15848v2)
- **Branchat** (2026) is a tree-structured interface for revisiting long conversations. A within-subject study with 12 participants against a linear chat baseline reportedly found faster completion, lower cognitive load, and higher trust and self-efficacy, described as "preliminary." *Verified only from the ACM listing as summarized in search; the full text returned 403.* [EA, likely CHI '26 late-breaking][V-partial] — [Li et al. 2026, "Branchat: A Tree-Structured Interface for Efficient Revisitation in Long-Horizon LLM Conversations," ACM, doi:10.1145/3772363.3798792](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3798792)
- **ChatGraPhT / ChatGPTree** is a design-inquiry paper in which any response can be branched. Each branch sends the model only its own path back to the root, and the authors argue that linear chat hides the shape of a user's reasoning. [PP][V-snippet] — [arXiv:2512.22790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22790)
- **Conversation Tree Architecture** organizes conversations as trees of context-isolated nodes, motivated by "logical context poisoning" in a single unbounded context. This is an architecture proposal, not a user study. [PP][V-snippet] — [arXiv:2603.21278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21278)
- **ExploreLLM** compared ChatGPT with a structured task-tree UI on trip planning (N = 8, qualitative, think-aloud). Seven of eight participants called ChatGPT's responses verbose or generic, and users had to "dig back through to find the interesting parts" of a long history ("nice not have a wall of text to have to look back through"). The reduced-load claims rest on participant comments; load was not measured. [EA][V] — [Ma et al. 2024, "Beyond ChatBots: ExploreLLM for Structured Thoughts and Personalized Model Responses," CHI EA '24, doi:10.1145/3613905.3651093](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613905.3651093); [arXiv:2312.00763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00763)
- **Sensecape** notes that "current interfaces for interacting with LLMs are generally linear to support conversational interaction," while complex information tasks need nonlinear, spatial work. In a within-subject study, users explored more topics and structured their knowledge hierarchically. The system explicitly supports switching between Pirolli & Card's foraging and sensemaking loops. [PR][V] — [Suh, Min, Palani & Xia 2023, UIST '23, doi:10.1145/3586183.3606756](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11483)
- **Graphologue** turns LLM text into interactive node-link diagrams. It was motivated by a formative finding that long responses were hard to comprehend quickly. [PR][V-snippet] — [Jiang, Rayan, Dow & Xia 2023, UIST '23](https://hci.ucsd.edu/papers/graphologue.pdf)
- **OnGoal** observes that as multi-turn chats grow, users struggle to track progress toward their goals. Goal tracking inside chat (N = 20, writing task, versus baseline chat) led to less time and effort. [PR][V] — [Coscia, Guo, Koh & Endert 2025, UIST '25, arXiv:2508.21061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21061)

**Cognitive load, verbosity, and overload ("firehose")**
- In a study of real-world logs (about 43K finance and 41.5K health ChatGPT and Gemini conversations, July–October 2025), behavioural proxies for difficulty were repeated prompting and clarification requests. The effect of length depended on density. For finance clarification, longer responses predicted *less* clarification when information density was low and *more* when it was high, crossing over at about 150–200 words. Markdown density was associated with *more* repeated prompting (OR ≈ 1.1). The number of topics in a conversation did **not** predict breakdown. [PP][V] — [Wang, Bilal & Zaman 2026, "When AI Becomes Hard to Understand," arXiv:2609.17301](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.17301v1)
- In an education context, shorter chatbot responses "encouraged careful reading and reduced cognitive load," which helped learners detect logical fallacies, though not factual errors. [PR][V-snippet] — ["Supporting Learners' Use of Imperfect Generative Pedagogical Chatbots: The Role of Chatbot Response Uncertainty and Reduced Verbosity," ACM 2026, doi:10.1145/3772318.3791940](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791940)
- Practitioners inspect "10s to 100s" of LLM responses through chat, which is slow. A controlled comparison of a sensemaking tool against a linear baseline found **no difference in cognitive-load questions**. [PR][V-snippet] — [Gero, Swoopes, Gu, Kummerfeld & Glassman 2024, "Supporting Sensemaking of Large Language Model Outputs at Scale," CHI '24, doi:10.1145/3613904.3642139](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642139)
- LLMs reduced students' mental effort compared with web search, but they also reduced the depth of their scientific reasoning. [PR][V-title and snippet] — [Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024, "Cognitive ease at a cost," *Computers in Human Behavior* 160:108386](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/114673/114673.pdf)
- In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, GenAI reduced perceived effort, and higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking. [PR][B] — [Lee et al. 2025, "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking," CHI '25, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713778](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
- **Model-side analogue.** Top LLMs perform an average of **39% worse** in multi-turn than single-turn settings across six tasks, because they make early assumptions and over-rely on them. The linear accumulation of context degrades the *model* too. Reportedly ICLR 2026, unconfirmed. [PP][V] — [Laban, Hayashi, Zhou & Neville 2025, "LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation," arXiv:2505.06120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)

**Information foraging and sensemaking with LLMs**
- Information Foraging Theory models people as maximizing information gained per unit of cost while moving between "patches." [PR][B] — [Pirolli & Card 1999, "Information Foraging," *Psychological Review* 106(4):643–675](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643)
- IFT "has not yet been adopted or validated" for "non-patchy LLM-based chatbot environments." This paper only offers hypotheses as a thought experiment. [PP][V] — [Srinivasa Ragavan & Alipour 2024, "Revisiting Human Information Foraging: Adaptations for LLM-based Chatbots," arXiv:2406.04452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04452)
- A related argument says generative AI shifts work from information *foraging* to information *farming*. [PP; reportedly SIGIR 2026][V-snippet] — [Azzopardi & Roegiest 2026, "Information Farming," arXiv:2601.12544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12544)

**Product signal**
- OpenAI shipped "Branch in new chat" in ChatGPT around **4 September 2025**. It forks a conversation from any message into a new thread that keeps prior context, and it launched first on the web. [PRESS][V] — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/chatgpt/chatgpt-has-added-a-great-new-feature-and-its-one-i-know-ill-be-using-a-lot); [BGR](https://bgr.com/1959993/chatgpt-branch-conversations-new-feature-now-available/); I found no official OpenAI announcement in the results.

### Inferences
- The convergent qualitative finding across independent teams (Munich, UCSD, Google, Georgia Tech) is the same: scroll-buried context, improvised forking, and losing track of goals. That consistency is stronger than any single study.
- The *quantitative* case that tree or canvas UIs reduce cognitive load is weak. Branchat is preliminary (N = 12), Gero et al. found a null, and CanvasConvo has no linear-chat control. The report should present branching as addressing a well-documented *navigation and organization* problem, with load reduction plausible but not yet demonstrated.
- CanvasConvo's finding that users were unsure what context a branch inherits is an important design caution. Branching UIs add a new mental-model burden, so "non-linear is natural" does not mean "non-linear UI is free."
- The logs study's null result on topic count is a mild counterpoint. Multi-topic conversations were not associated with more breakdown, at least by those proxies.

### Gaps
- **I found no peer-reviewed or preprint log study of how often or why users use edit/regenerate (implicit branching) in ChatGPT or Claude.** The closest is an EMNLP 2025 analysis of follow-up messages in Bing Copilot and WildChat logs ([aclanthology 2025.emnlp-main.852](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/pdf/emnlp/2025.emnlp-main.852.pdf); title not verified).
- I found no study that measures how many follow-up questions users *intend* but never ask after a long LLM answer, which is the direct "firehose" phenomenon.
- I found no randomized comparison of tree versus linear chat with objective load measures (dual-task, physiological) and N > 30.
- "Firehose problem" appears to be practitioner vocabulary. I found no academic paper using the term.

## 5. Constructal law (Bejan) applied to conversation, discourse, or knowledge; and the "tools for thought" design argument

### Takeaway
**The constructal link is metaphorical only.** Bejan's constructal law (1996/97) says that finite flow systems evolve configurations that give easier access to what flows, which produces tree-shaped architectures such as river basins and lungs. Bejan has extended it to "knowledge" and to the spread of ideas, using citation curves. But I found **no** peer-reviewed or preprint work applying constructal law to conversation structure, discourse, hypertext, or chat interfaces. The design-side "tools for thought" writers (Appleton, Wattenberger, Matuschak) argue against chat-as-default from craft and experience, not from data. Matuschak argues for *networks over hierarchies*, which cuts against a strict-tree framing.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin.** Constructal theory started with tree-shaped conducting paths for cooling a heat-generating volume. [PR][B] — [Bejan 1997, *Int. J. Heat and Mass Transfer* 40(4):799–816](https://doi.org/10.1016/0017-9310(96)00175-5)
- **Statement.** A flow system evolves its configuration over time to provide easier access to its currents. [PR][B] — [Bejan & Lorente 2010, "The constructal law of design and evolution in nature," *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 365(1545):1335–1347](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2009.0302); [Duke MEMS summary](https://mems.duke.edu/impact/research/energy/bejan-constructal-law/)
- **Tree-shaped flows in biology.** [PR][V-link] — [Bejan 2005, "The constructal law of organization in nature: tree-shaped flows and body size," *J. Experimental Biology* 208(9):1677–1686](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/208/9/1677/9374/The-constructal-law-of-organization-in-nature-tree)
- **Ideas as flow (closest application).** Bejan & Lorente argue that citation histories (S-shaped curves, a rising h-index, a falling m-quotient) follow from how an idea "flows over a populated territory." The idea moves fast and long, like "convection" along established channels of researchers, and slow and short, like "diffusion" sideways to new users. This concerns idea *diffusion through populations*, not discourse structure. [PR][V] — [Bejan & Lorente 2012, "The physics of spreading ideas," *Int. J. Heat and Mass Transfer* 55(4):802–807, doi:10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2011.10.029](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/760133)
- **Knowledge as flow (popular book).** In a popular book, knowledge, ideas, and data appear among the things that "flow." Universities, newspapers, and books are cast as knowledge-flow systems, and knowledge is described as "design change" that keeps flows easier and longer-lived. [Trade book][V via secondary sources] — Bejan & Zane 2012, *Design in Nature*, Doubleday; [Goodreads listing](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13132411-design-in-nature); [Big Think explainer](https://bigthink.com/surprising-science/constructal-law/) [PRESS]
- **Hierarchy and science.** Hierarchy in nature, society, and science is attributed to the constructal law, and the spreading and diversification of engineering disciplines from the 1700s to the 2000s is offered as an example. [Book][V via reviews] — [Bejan 2020, *Freedom and Evolution: Hierarchy in Nature, Society and Science*, Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-34009-4_9); [ASME J. Energy Resources Technology review](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/energyresources/article/143/8/086501/1106102/Freedom-and-Evolution-Hierarchy-in-Nature-Society)
- **Social systems.** Constructal theory has also been applied to social organization (cities, economics, social dynamics). [Edited book][B] — [Bejan & Merkx (eds.) 2007, *Constructal Theory of Social Dynamics*, Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-47681-0)
- **Negative result.** Searches combining "constructal" with language, discourse, hypertext, knowledge management, or conversation returned only unrelated "construction grammar" and Pask's Conversation Theory results. **No application of constructal law to discourse or conversation was found.** [V-negative] — [search conducted 2026-11-05; representative non-matches e.g. Cambridge *Language and Cognition*, "Conversation, Construction Grammar, and cognition"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/article/conversation-construction-grammar-and-cognition/F60C5CDB2335B915DD69D2C6E7A1C903)
- **Criticism.** Secondary commentary describes the law as very broad or vague. Treat its knowledge-flow extensions as Bejan's own extrapolation, not tested science. [low-quality secondary][V] — [Continuing Creation commentary](https://continuingcreation.org/professor-bejans-constructal-law/)
- **A non-metaphorical alternative.** For a peer-reviewed claim that *efficiency pressures shape linguistic form*, the established literature is information-theoretic efficiency, not constructal law. [PR][B] — [Gibson et al. 2019, "How efficiency shapes human language," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 23(5):389–407](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.02.003)

**Tools-for-thought / design argument (all BLOG)**
- **Maggie Appleton** argues that chat is the obvious default but "the lazy solution," and that "we are irreversibly anchored to this text-heavy, turn-based interface paradigm." She prototypes non-chat alternatives: background "daemons" that comment in a writing environment, a cause-and-effect "Branches" explorer, and a right-click reasoning menu. *Note:* the essay does **not** explicitly argue about cognitive load or conversational branching, and her "Branches" prototype is about causal chains, not conversation trees. It appears to date from mid-2023 (third-party mentions start 14 June 2023). [BLOG][V] — [Appleton, "Language Model Sketchbook, or Why I Hate Chatbots"](https://maggieappleton.com/lm-sketchbook)
- **Amelia Wattenberger** argues that chat has no "working buffer," so comparing iterations means scrolling, and "it's laborious to figure out what concretely has changed." Each reply forces a switch between implementing and evaluating, which breaks flow: "Good tools let the user choose when to switch between implementation and evaluation." [BLOG, 2023][V] — [Wattenberger, "Why Chatbots Are Not the Future of Interfaces"](https://wattenberger.com/thoughts/boo-chatbots)
- **Andy Matuschak** advises "let structure emerge organically." Hierarchical taxonomies misrepresent knowledge because items belong in several places and relate across branches, so associative networks are preferable. [BLOG][V] — [Matuschak, "Prefer associative ontologies to hierarchical taxonomies"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Prefer_associative_ontologies_to_hierarchical_taxonomies); background: [Matuschak & Nielsen 2019, "How can we develop transformative tools for thought?"](https://numinous.productions/ttft/) [essay][B]

### Inferences
- There is a genuine *structural* parallel, but it is only a parallel. In Roberts-style QUD trees, answers to subquestions "drain" upward into the superquestion, much as tributaries feed a river toward one outlet. A constructal reading would say that conversation grows sub-questions in order to move understanding to the root more easily. That is an analogy. Nobody has defined measurable "flow," "resistance," or an "access" objective for discourse. The report should call it a metaphor or an "invitation," not evidence.
- Two lines of evidence cut against any strict-tree image, whether constructal or not: the empirical discourse findings (Wolf & Gibson's crossed dependencies, Ginzburg's partially ordered QUD) and Matuschak's design argument (networks over trees). Real conversational and knowledge structure is better described as "mostly tree, with cross-links."
- The design writers are useful for *framing* and *vocabulary*: no working buffer, forced context switches, chat as the lazy default. They should be cited as practitioner argument, not as findings.

### Gaps
- I found no constructal-law application to discourse, dialogue, hypertext, or UI design, peer-reviewed or otherwise. If one exists, it is obscure.
- I found no peer-reviewed critique specifically addressing constructal law's extensions to knowledge or social systems. The only critical comment found is in low-quality secondary commentary.
- I did not verify the exact text of *Design in Nature*'s knowledge-flow passages; they are known here only through secondary summaries.
